Where every input came from, and what it costs when one is wrong

The Rietveld refinement was perhaps a third of the work on those five patterns. The rest was scaffolding — peak finding, autoindexing, phase screening, repository archaeology and TOPAS macro reading — and none of it reports an uncertainty. Four of its assumptions are measured here. One of them withdraws a claim from the first report.

No structure database was consulted at any point. Not OPTIMADE — which is what guiLLeMot itself was built around and what the empty cifs/ directory is waiting for — not the COD, not Materials Project, not the ICSD. Every structure is either transcribed from a TOPAS file in the repository, measured off the pattern, or recalled. The last category is the one worth auditing.

Reading the repository — the highest-yield tool, and the dullest

gh api, a shallow clone, and grep

no refinement involved decisive

The single most valuable finding in the whole job came from grep. The NaCoO2/ folder holds a TOPAS input whose xdd line names NaCoO2_CuKa_XRD.xy — a file that does not exist in the repository. Four lines from the bottom it writes Out_CIF_STR("KD1-2_riet_01.cif"). That single string is what pairs the orphaned input to the KD1-2_5 pattern and turns an un-analysable file into a full four-site structure with ionic form factors and a texture model.

Second-most valuable, equally dull: byte-comparing the 2θ columns. Three of the five patterns share an identical grid — 5.01436° to 89.99116° in 5086 points — which is what licenses carrying one .inp's instrument description onto two patterns that have none. Without that, the Kα2 ratio, the polarisation convention and the axial model would all have been guesses on MnSb_34 and HL2-1.

The autoindexer — and the claim it withdraws

Pair-seeded two-parameter indexing

~60 lines; no DICVOL, TREOR or McMaille involved works, but narrower than the conclusion drawn from it

For a two-parameter metric Q = A·M + C·l², any two observed Q values plus two trial (M, l) labels determine (A, C) by a 2×2 solve. Enumerate that over the nine strongest peaks and a label set, keep positive solutions, score by the share of observed peak prominence indexed. Cubic is the one-parameter case. It recovers MnSb_34's true cell — a = 4.1417, c = 5.7803 Å — at 82 %, which is what made 68 % on HL2-1 look like a failure.

But it only has cubic, tetragonal and hexagonal. To find out what that costs, I fed it the exact, complete peak lists of single-phase compounds of lower symmetry, generated from their space groups so the systematic absences are right:

Correction to the first report

The first report described HL2-1 as “unidentified, ≥ 2 phases” and “Unknown, at least two phases.” That claim is withdrawn. A single-phase orthorhombic compound produces exactly the signature I read as multiphase, and this tooling cannot separate the two.

What survives: HL2-1 is not cubic, tetragonal or hexagonal. Whether it is a mixture or one low-symmetry phase is open. The Pawley evidence in the first report — 296 free intensities and whole peaks still unfitted — is untouched, but it only ever showed that that particular hexagonal cell is wrong, not how many phases are present. The first report has been updated.

Two caveats on the figure itself. The synthetic peak lists are complete, while the measured HL2-1 list is threshold-limited to 74 peaks, so the comparison is indicative rather than exact. And the indexer refines no zero-shift and uses a hit-fraction rather than a proper M₂₀ / F₂₀, both of which a real indexing program would do.

The phase screen — does it find an answer it is known to contain?

36 cells, position matching, no structure factors

reflections via pxrdref.crystallography.symmetry validated on a known answer

The cells are recalled, but the reflection lists are not: they come from the package's own generator, so systematic absences are handled by gemmi rather than by me. The screen's real weakness is that it matches positions and ignores intensity — so a candidate with a big cell indexes everything and means nothing. The defence is a second column: what fraction of the candidate's own predicted lines are actually seen.

A screen that cannot find a known answer cannot be read as evidence of absence, so I pointed it at MnSb_34, where the answer is MnSb + Sb:

Read the first column alone and the screen fails — NaSb tops it with 390 predicted lines and only 9 % of them present. Read both columns and the truth separates cleanly: the two real phases are the only entries with a high lines-seen fraction and a small predicted-line count. So the screen works, on the joint criterion, and nothing in HL2-1 passes it.

What that licenses is narrow: none of these 36 phases is a major component of HL2-1. It is not a statement about anything outside the library, and the library is 36 cells I wrote from memory covering the chemistry of the neighbouring folders. Adding calculated |F|² would make the first column mean something on its own and is the obvious upgrade.

Three readings, in order of how much they matter.

  • The cell had to be recalled to better than about 2 %. Outside that the refinement does not rescue it — it drives the minor phase to its bounds and reports 0.0–0.2 wt %, i.e. it silently concludes there is no Sb. The only warning is Rwp going 0.5–0.6 points worse, which on a 1.7 wt % impurity is easy to attribute to something else.
  • z is not measured at all. Starting from 0.2335 it lands at 0.2358; starting from 0.2663 it stays at 0.2642. Same Rwp to four figures, same cell to five, same weight fraction. Whatever the first report implies by quoting a refined structure, this coordinate carries no information in this pattern.
  • The refined c deserves a flag the first report did not give it. 11.3256(115) Å against the A7 literature 11.2740 is +0.46 %, or 4.5 σ. It is reproducible — the fit returns to it from a −3.3 % start — so it is not a starting artefact. Either the Sb is strained or alloyed, or a two-line identification does not pin c as well as the esd suggests. On the evidence here I cannot tell which, and the weight fraction should be read as ~2 %, not 1.66(28) %.

Worth being clear about what does hold: the identification itself does not rest on memory. The two impurity lines were matched at d = 3.114 and 2.154 against A7 Sb's (012) at 3.111 and (110) at 2.154 before any refinement, and the space group came from the neighbouring .inp. It is the quantification that needed the cell to be right.

The goniometer radius — an assumption with no error bar

217.5 mm, stated nowhere

FeSb_19RBM refit at six radii systematic, unreported by any esd

Converting Simple_Axial_Model(!axial, 10) into FCJ ratios needs a radius: S/L = H/L = (10 mm / 2) / R. I used 217.5 mm because it is pxrd-refine's default. No file in the repository states it, and lab diffractometers ship anywhere from ~180 to ~320 mm. R enters twice — through those axial ratios and through the displacement aberration Δ2θ = −(2s/R)·cos θ — so I varied it consistently in both and refit.

our reported 1σ on a64 ppm

Rwp across the whole range0.029 pts
displacement absorbs0.020 → 0.091 mm
The data cannot tell you the radius. Rwp moves by 0.029 points across the entire plausible range — nothing. What moves instead is the specimen displacement, by a factor of 4.6, soaking up almost the whole difference. The residue lands on the cell as a systematic worth roughly ±85 ppm, which is larger than the refinement's own esd and the same size as the agreement with TOPAS that the first report treated as its headline result.

None of which makes that agreement wrong — both codes are near 4.1134 Å and the difference really is ~100 ppm. It means the cross-check is demonstrating that two implementations of the same physics agree, not that the cell is known to 100 ppm. Quoting a lab cell to better than about ±100 ppm needs the goniometer radius as a datum, or an internal standard, neither of which this dataset has.

Two tools that needed no audit

Peak extraction, and looking at the plots

scipy.signal.find_peaks · pxrdref.viz.plot_for_vlm

Peak extraction is find_peaks on (data − rolling-minimum background), with a three-point parabolic interpolation of each maximum so positions are not quantised to the 0.0167° step. That crude background is used only for finding peaks; every refinement uses the package's own automatic Chebyshev selection instead. It is checkable by eye against the annotated plot, and it was.

Looking at the plots is not a metaphor — the diagnostic montage auto-zooms the four worst-misfit regions and titles each with its own numbers, and I read those images back. The MnSb_33 diagnosis in the first report — sharp cores on broad bases, a bimodal domain-size population rather than a width to be refined — came from seeing that shape. No agreement index would have said it: Rwp, GoF and Durbin–Watson all just report “bad”, and the Stephens block was happy to fit it with unphysical coefficients.

What I would change

  • Query OPTIMADE for the structures. guiLLeMot already has the tool and the repository has an empty cifs/ directory waiting for the output. That removes the whole “from memory” row of the ledger, including the 2 %-wide convergence trap the Sb phase turned out to sit in.
  • Put |F|² into the phase screen. One column doing the work of two, and NaSb stops topping a list it has no business being on.
  • Ask for the goniometer radius — it is one number from whoever collected the data, and it is worth more than any amount of further refinement on these patterns.
  • Extend the indexer to three parameters, or use a real one. The two-parameter version cost a wrong sentence in the first report, which is exactly the kind of error a tool with no error bar produces.
